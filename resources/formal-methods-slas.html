ZENITH

Formal methods and executable SLAs

A look ahead at verification and efficiency

Return to Operational efficiency · Facilitator notes

The opportunity

Precisely defined commitments can make some checking and acceptance work automatic. Wider use of formal methods and executable service-level agreements could reduce repeated manual verification and coordination. This is a potential efficiency gain, not a measured saving for this workshop.

What each contributes

  • Formal methods: establish that software satisfies specified properties under explicit assumptions. Proofs apply to what has actually been specified and verified.
  • Executable SLA conditions: express measurable service commitments as checks that software can evaluate. Passing a check can trigger an agreed acceptance, notification or other action. An executable check is not automatically a formal proof.

Example: An agreement requires every allocation to name an eligible worker and avoid scheduling conflicts. Code checks the allocation against agreed records. Formal verification could establish that an acceptance component never accepts an allocation that violates those encoded rules, assuming its inputs and dependencies meet the specification.

Impact on fees and operating costs

Assessment: The clearest opportunity is to reduce recurring SLA administration and the effort needed to establish what happened. Legal fees may fall where clearer rules and evidence prevent disputes or shorten their investigation. Initial drafting, implementation and assurance costs can increase. The sources below do not establish a general percentage saving in legal fees.

The following are potential cost effects inferred from the mechanisms, not measured outcomes:

Monitoring, evidence collection and credit processing — potential recurring savings
Agreed measurements and automated checks can reduce manual reconciliation, claim preparation and routine acceptance work. Much of this is operations or finance labor, not legal fees.
Drafting and negotiation — more work initially; reuse may help later
Parties must specify metrics, exclusions, evidence sources, remedies and the relationship between code and written terms. Reusing reviewed terms and components may reduce later work when the transaction fits the same assumptions.
Disputes and legal advice — possible savings, not elimination
Shared records can narrow disagreements about performance. Disputes about consent, interpretation, faulty measurements, exceptions or remedies can remain and may require legal and technical expertise.
Engineering and assurance fees — added investment
Specification, implementation, proof work, integration, testing and maintenance cost money. Compare these costs with the recurring work actually avoided. NIST describes how specifications can reduce some testing effort while noting that testing is still needed alongside proofs. Source: NIST.
Service fees and credits — no automatic reduction in the price of service
An automatic credit transfers value from provider to customer under the agreement. It is not itself a reduction in total processing cost. More complete credit enforcement may benefit customers while increasing provider payouts; any administrative savings are separate.

Where the case is strongest: repeated transactions with stable, objectively measurable commitments, trusted evidence and agreed remedies. One-off or subjective agreements may not recover the additional specification and assurance cost. Measure staff hours, legal invoices, disputes and technical maintenance before claiming a saving.

Legal position and the scope of a guarantee

In British Columbia, section 15(2) of the Electronic Transactions Act prevents a contract from being invalid or unenforceable solely because electronic information was used in its formation. Section 16 permits formation through electronic agents. These provisions recognize the medium; they do not validate every term or make a checker’s output conclusive. Section 17 also addresses certain material errors in dealings with electronic agents.

A proof can establish that a credit calculator follows specified rules, or that an acceptance component rejects outputs violating specified conditions. It does not by itself prove future uptime, the truth of external measurements or fulfilment of every legal obligation. Executable conditions can be automated without formal proof; formal methods add assurance about specified behavior.

For an actual SLA, have the legal and technical specification reviewed together. Set out which version governs, what prevails if code and prose conflict, who supplies evidence, how exceptions and corrections work, and how changes and disputes are handled. This is a workshop design recommendation, not an opinion on a particular agreement’s enforceability.

The Law Commission’s analysis for England and Wales similarly identifies questions of formation, interpretation, remedies and jurisdiction for smart legal contracts. It is comparative context, not Canadian legal authority. Source: Law Commission.

A concrete SLA example

The Amazon Compute SLA ties service credits to defined availability measurements. Its credit-request process requires incident details and supporting logs, imposes a claim deadline and includes exclusions. It also provides a separate automatic no-charge provision for certain instance outages.

This illustrates work that an executable SLA could streamline: gathering agreed evidence, calculating eligibility and applying a credit. It also shows why “the check passed” is insufficient unless the check captures the relevant definitions and exceptions. This is an illustration of the opportunity, not a claim that AWS uses formal methods for this SLA or that customers can bypass its stated claim process.

Workshop wording

“Verifiable agreements can reduce the cost of checking performance and administering remedies. They may also reduce some dispute-related legal work. The opportunity is to reuse precise, reviewed rules across many transactions, while accounting for the cost of building and maintaining that assurance.”

The workshop focus

Begin with evaluation and evolution: check results, measure cost per accepted task and retain tested improvements. Include human effort, token consumption, tools and the cost of developing those improvements.
