ZENITH

Looking Forward

Slides

Summary

Explore what ontologies, service requests and connected work graphs could make possible, and how verifiable services, shared technical foundations and collaboration could create lasting business value. Identify where these ideas could help participants’ organizations.

Business relevance

Reduce duplicated effort, retain operating knowledge, make service commitments more dependable and cooperate while preserving business independence.

Intended outcome

Participants identify a priority activity to improve, its expected business value and a practical next step.

Bulleted plan

  • Ontology — understand the business independently of its applications
    • Describe the information, responsibilities, decisions and functions the business needs.
    • Examine an existing application or process and reconstruct the requirements it serves.
    • Identify which capabilities to retain, replace, build or obtain as services.
    • Preserve business meaning and operating knowledge as implementations change.
    • The opportunity is to remove the necessity of renting a particular application to operate the business. Paid software can remain a deliberate choice.
    • An ontology helps describe what a replacement must do; implementation, migration, testing and maintenance still require work.

    Your business should be defined by what it does, rather than by the applications it happens to use.

  • Service requests — coordinate relationships and unfamiliar work
    • Define what one person, agent or organization can request from another.
    • Establish required information, expected outcomes, authority and commitments.
    • Support clarification, negotiation, acceptance, rejection and changes of scope.
    • Handle novelty by recognizing when an existing service definition is insufficient and routing the request for judgment or a revised agreement.
    • Coordinate routine communication between environments while preserving the points where people must decide.
    • Explore a structure for working out what the work should be, similar to how people clarify a request before committing to it.

    Automation needs a way to ask, clarify and agree, as well as a way to execute.

  • DevGraph and connected business graphs — choose what to improve
    • Connect business goals and KPIs to proposals, projects, dependencies and tasks.
    • Compare candidate improvements against value, effort, constraints and uncertainty.
    • Business graph: responsibilities, services, information, relationships and available capabilities.
    • Work graphs: proposed changes and the work undertaken to achieve particular outcomes.
    • Relate each participant’s work graph to the responsibilities and services around it.
    • Connect completed work to its context, decisions, outcomes and reviewed corrections.
    • Explore a connected view across independently controlled environments. Each participant shares the information needed for an interaction, subject to access rules.
    • Use the resulting context and evidence to develop model evaluations, harness evaluations and improvement experiments.

    The business can use evidence from its work to choose its next improvement.

  • Evaluations — improve models, harnesses and the whole process
    • Model: can it make the required distinction or produce an acceptable result?
    • Harness and environment: did it receive the right context, tools, instructions and authority?
    • Whole process: did the business achieve a useful outcome at an acceptable cost?
    • Proposed change: does the new approach improve performance without introducing unacceptable regressions?
    • Explore how connected business and work graphs could organize the context and evidence for answering these questions.
    • Useful evaluations still require meaningful cases, acceptance criteria and reliable observations.
  • Describe, coordinate, improve
    • Describe what your business needs.
    • Establish how others can contribute.
    • Use the results to decide what to improve next.
    • Connect these possibilities to the existing Looking Forward topics: formal service commitments describe dependable interfaces; the commons supports reusable implementations; collaboration connects independently directed businesses.

    These are opportunities to explore; their scope and delivery readiness remain to determine.

  • Formal Method SLA — clearer service commitments
    • Explore connecting service commitments to precisely stated properties, verification evidence and operating assumptions.
    • Machine interoperability: Explore how shared, machine-readable service specifications and SLA terms could let different tools and agents exchange requests, interpret results and check verification evidence against the same commitments.
    • Discuss what a provider promises, what can be checked and who remains accountable.
    • Connect this to confidence in delegating work and evaluating suppliers.
  • Technology Commons — shared foundations, retained value
    • Introduce jointly developing and maintaining reusable tools, specifications and infrastructure.
    • Consider what businesses could share while retaining control of their information, specialized knowledge and decisions.
    • Connect shared investment to reduced duplication and access to capabilities.
  • Trustless vs Consortium — choosing how to establish trust
    • Compare arrangements that minimize reliance on trusted parties with arrangements governed by identified participants.
    • Consider what must be trusted, what can be verified and how responsibility and disputes are handled.
    • Connect these choices to practical business relationships and coordination costs.
  • Super-collaboration vs Competition — exploring the opportunity
    • Present the working hypothesis that lowering coordination costs and making useful work reusable could create advantages over repeatedly developing capabilities in isolation.
    • Explore how cooperation on shared foundations can coexist with competition in products, expertise and service.
    • Consider how contributors receive value and how shared foundations are maintained.
  • Five Activities worksheet — locating the value
    • List the five most time-consuming recurring activities in the organization.
    • Record who is involved, frequency, estimated total staff time and sources of effort or delay. Rough estimates are sufficient.
    • Select one activity worth investigating and explain what improvement would matter.
  • Choose a practical next step
    • Consider what to improve internally, obtain as a service or pursue collaboratively.
    • Identify a small experiment or investigation, the support needed and how its value would be assessed.

Evidence of success

Participants leave with five candidate activities, one reasoned priority, an expected benefit and a feasible next step.

Time

Up to 10 minutes.
