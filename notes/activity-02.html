ZENITH

Foundations · Facilitator notes

Activity outline

Define the structures that let people develop their own working environments, retain useful knowledge and coordinate through clear responsibilities, interfaces and access.

Data foundations — making work understandable across boundaries

The problem: People repeatedly reconcile applications, find missing information and explain context before work can begin.

  • Each person needs an evolving working environment suited to their responsibilities, with well-defined interfaces, responsibilities and access.
  • Introduce the core ontology as shared structures and vocabulary for describing the business, its relationships and the interfaces between working environments.
  • Begin with inputs → function doing the work → outputs.
  • Describe the function’s purpose, required information, operating logic and promised result. Make responsibility and authority explicit.
  • Expand the model to expose dependencies, handoffs, decisions and exceptions.
  • Let people retain and develop knowledge within their own environments while making the information required for an interaction explicit.

Decision making — choosing how work is performed

The problem: Routine choices consume attention; similar requests receive inconsistent treatment; exceptions depend on someone remembering the rules.

  • Open the decision black box: identify its inputs, available choices, decision and assessment criteria.
  • Introduce algorithmic methods, LLMs and System One models alongside human judgment and approval. Define “System One models” before using the term in the presentation.
  • Show how reviewed examples, choices and corrections can help systems learn to approximate how we make decisions.
  • Capture the context and reasons behind corrections. Feedback requires a mechanism for updating rules, instructions, examples or models.
  • Introduce graph algorithms for suitable optimization problems, including routing, matching and dependency ordering.
  • Combine approaches deliberately: interpret a request, calculate options and approve exceptions.
  • Distinguish learning to imitate existing decisions from improving those decisions.

Provenance — knowing what happened and why

The problem: When a result is challenged—or needed for improvement—the business cannot easily reconstruct which information, rule, model or approval produced it.

  • Identify authoritative information and its sources.
  • Connect a request to the information, rules, decisions, actions and results it produced.
  • Record who or what performed the work, which versions were used and where approval occurred.
  • Preserve corrections and exceptions so results can be inspected, investigated and used in improvement.

Evaluation — finding weaknesses and checking improvements

The problem: The business needs to discover where models struggle, identify whether the model or operating environment needs changing, and determine whether those changes improve performance.

  • Build evaluation cases around each user’s responsibilities, working context, intended results and observed failures.
  • Define the intended result and acceptance criteria for each function. Assess individual outputs and the complete process.
  • Consider correctness, usefulness, time, cost, rework and adherence to permissions.
  • Capture failures and reviewed corrections with enough context to investigate what needs to change: the model, information, instructions, tools, rules or assignment of work.
  • Compare proposed changes with the current version under comparable conditions, using reviewed examples and execution evidence.
  • Keep evaluation cases separate from examples used for training or tuning, and check whether improvements introduce regressions elsewhere.
  • Identify what can be checked automatically and what requires human judgment. Retain evaluation history to inform adoption decisions.

Evolution — retaining improvements

The problem: Staff correct the same mistakes repeatedly, while useful corrections fail to become retained improvements to processes, tools or models.

  • Capture feedback, corrections and failures as candidates for improvement within each person’s environment.
  • Introduce evolutionary harnesses: observe → propose a change → evaluate → approve or reject → retain the approved version.
  • Apply changes to SOPs, rules, instructions, implementations, models or assignments.
  • Identify who may propose, evaluate and approve changes, including changes affecting shared interfaces, responsibilities or authority.
  • Preserve version history and the ability to return to a previous approach.
  • Connect this cycle to making effective use of Hermes within the business’s operating rules.

Access — defining who may do what

The problem: Giving someone a task doesn’t establish what they may disclose, change or commit the business to—or when they must ask.

  • Specify which people, software, agents and providers may read, change, request, share or approve.
  • Distinguish access to information from authority to act or make commitments.
  • Define permissions for processing, retention, reuse and training where relevant.
  • Connect assignment to both capability and authority, including when the work must return to a person for approval.
  • Distinguish permissions described in the model from controls enforced by software.

A business that can learn from its work

Connect the foundations through a cycle: describe → decide and act → record → evaluate → improve → repeat.

  • Data foundations: describe the business, its tasks and relationships.
  • Decision making: choose how work is performed.
  • Provenance: preserve what happened and the context behind it.
  • Evaluation: assess models and environments to guide training and improvement.
  • Evolution: test changes and retain approved improvements.
  • Access: define how each working environment interacts with others and with shared resources, including who may observe, act, evaluate, change and approve. Each retains its own space, evolving around its responsibilities.

Takeaway: Each completed task can contribute evidence for improving how the business operates—while the business retains control over its knowledge, decisions and changes.

Transition: Participants will build a small instance of this structure in the practical work.
