Our purpose

Our mission is to ensure access to information, knowledge, and tools for everybody, not just a select few.

We’re entering an age where small businesses—and individuals—can stand on the same technological foundations as the world’s largest corporations.

Our responsibility is to help make that possibility practical: the ability to build, improve and collaborate while retaining sovereignty over what you create and the ability to choose who you share and build with.

Data strategy and foundations

Show that effective adoption depends on understandable, reliable and appropriately accessible business knowledge. Introduce shared definitions and clear information boundaries as the foundation for coordinating work.

Continue the map example: a useful map identifies what places are, what they contain and how they relate. The structure of business information determines whether people and agents can navigate it.

Ask: what does someone—or an agent—need to know to do this task correctly?

  • Identify the knowledge the work depends on. Records, procedures, relationships, rules and the experience people carry.
  • Establish shared meaning. Agree on what a customer, request, task or completed result means across the business. Explain canonical shared meaning as agreed definitions and relationships that people and systems can consistently refer to.
  • Make authoritative information identifiable. Know its source, who maintains it and whether it is current.
  • Define access and use. Decide what people, agents and partners may see, change and share.
  • Keep knowledge usable as tools change. Preserve the information, context and relationships the business needs.

Example: Before an agent can prepare a customer quote, it needs to know which customer record is authoritative, which pricing rules apply, what commitments it may make and who approves exceptions.

Introduce ontology as a name for the shared definitions and relationships. Develop its structure in Foundations.

Reliability and verifiability

Strong data foundations open the door to modelling, interpretability, evals, evolutionary optimization of the working environment, verifiability and formal methods. These give us ways to understand and improve reliability.

This is where the discussion can become complex. Keep the workshop focused on evals, with a brief look ahead at stronger guarantees. Formal methods already exist; applying them across a business’s AI workflows is a more ambitious direction.

Evals: the practical focus

An eval is a repeatable check of whether a system meets an agreed expectation. Define the result, test representative requests and exceptions, and inspect what actually happened. Use separate examples to assess changes to prompts, tools, procedures and the working environment before retaining them. Passing the examples is evidence of performance on those checks, not proof of every future outcome.

Example: For allocating a service request, check that the assigned person has the required skills, is available and has no conflicting allocation. If no eligible person is available, the expected result is an explicit escalation.

Formal methods and service agreements

Formal methods can establish that software satisfies precisely stated properties under explicit assumptions. Measurable parts of a service-level agreement (SLA) can be expressed as machine-checkable conditions. A deliverable can then be checked against those conditions, and software can automate agreed acceptance or other actions when the checks succeed.

The guarantee applies to the specified property within the verified model and its assumptions. The specification, evidence about external events and connection to the deployed system still matter. This does not automatically establish that every business or legal obligation has been fulfilled.

Frame reduced verification and dispute-handling effort as a potential benefit. Do not claim that formal methods eliminate all legal fees: coded agreements still raise questions of formation, interpretation, remedies and jurisdiction.

Transition to efficiency: Once we can verify a good result, how can we produce it with less effort?

  • NIST: Combinatorial methods for trust and assurance — why testing remains necessary alongside proofs and implementation assumptions.

Operational efficiency

Core message: mapping out a process and tracking its execution lets us see where it can be improved. When the inputs, decision rules and success conditions are explicit, we can automate decisions and routing within that process and verify the result.

Map and track

Continue the map example: identify the steps, their dependencies, who or what performs them, the decisions between them, and the evidence needed to call the work complete. Track actual executions against that map, including exceptions. A process diagram describes intended work; the record of execution shows what happened.

For each service request, keep a case ID, the step, its start and finish, the decision and rule version used, where it was routed, and the outcome of the check. This lets us distinguish waiting from execution, spot repeated handoffs and identify work that comes back for correction.

Two types of improvement

Cost minimization: use fewer resources to run the same process and achieve the same quality. For example, use less context or a less expensive tool for a step whose result can still be checked.

Process optimization: change how the work is organized so it scales better. For example, replace repeatedly searching every record with a maintained index that supports efficient lookup. Include the work of building and updating that index.

The technical distinction is lower cost for the same growth pattern versus a lower asymptotic growth rate. Define what grows—requests, records or another input—and what work is counted. A cheaper or faster run alone does not prove better scaling; not every process change alters the complexity class. On the slide, keep this to “lower the cost” and “improve how it scales.” Both must preserve the required outcome.

Evaluation and evolution

Choose a measurable improvement: fewer incorrect assignments, less waiting, fewer repeated steps or more requests completed correctly the first time. Use the execution record to identify a change, test it on comparable requests and keep it only when the evidence supports it. Check completion quality alongside speed.

The change might be a better intake form, a clearer rule, a different route, a connected tool or improved agent instructions. Harness evolution is one way to improve a step within this larger process.

Automate where success can be verified

For a service request, check that required information is present, apply the approved routing rule, send it to an eligible team and verify that the assignment was recorded and acknowledged. Missing information, ambiguous rules or a failed check go to review. A correct assignment is success for the dispatch step; completing the service requires its own evidence.

Automation requires connected tools and permission to act as well as a process map. Start with decisions whose inputs and outcomes can be checked, and continue recording the automated path so it can be evaluated and improved.

The longer-term formal methods and executable SLAs discussion extends the verification theme.

Orientation

Start with a recurring task from your own business.

  • Use one prepared SOP; rough notes are enough to begin.
  • Build a small instance of the structures introduced in Foundations.
  • Identify people and agent roles, information boundaries, permissions and review criteria.
  • Aim to leave with a useful business decision and a practical next step.
