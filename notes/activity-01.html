Our purpose

Our mission is to ensure access to information, knowledge, and tools for everybody, not just a select few.

We’re entering an age where small businesses—and individuals—can stand on the same technological foundations as the world’s largest corporations.

Our responsibility is to help make that possibility practical: the ability to build, improve and collaborate while retaining sovereignty over what you create and the ability to choose who you share and build with.

Data strategy and foundations

Show that effective adoption depends on understandable, reliable and appropriately accessible business knowledge. Introduce shared definitions and clear information boundaries as the foundation for coordinating work.

Continue the map example: a useful map identifies what places are, what they contain and how they relate. The structure of business information determines whether people and agents can navigate it.

Ask: what does someone—or an agent—need to know to do this task correctly?

  • Identify the knowledge the work depends on. Records, procedures, relationships, rules and the experience people carry.
  • Establish shared meaning. Agree on what a customer, request, task or completed result means across the business. Explain canonical shared meaning as agreed definitions and relationships that people and systems can consistently refer to.
  • Make authoritative information identifiable. Know its source, who maintains it and whether it is current.
  • Define access and use. Decide what people, agents and partners may see, change and share.
  • Keep knowledge usable as tools change. Preserve the information, context and relationships the business needs.

Example: Before an agent can prepare a customer quote, it needs to know which customer record is authoritative, which pricing rules apply, what commitments it may make and who approves exceptions.

Introduce ontology as a name for the shared definitions and relationships. Develop its structure in Foundations.

Reliability and verifiability

Define what a good result looks like and how it can be inspected.

  • Make responsibilities, permissions and approval points explicit.
  • Keep evidence of the information, rules, decisions and actions behind a result.
  • Evaluate models and working environments to discover weaknesses and assess changes.
  • Distinguish permissions specified in a model from controls enforced by software.

View slide

Operational efficiency

Consider the effort required to reach an accepted result.

  • Look at time, cost, delays, review effort and rework.
  • Choose deliberately what people, established rules, software and AI should do.
  • Retain reviewed corrections so they can become improvements.
  • Consider how capacity can grow while keeping responsibilities and review manageable.

Orientation

Start with a recurring task from your own business.

  • Use one prepared SOP; rough notes are enough to begin.
  • Build a small instance of the structures introduced in Foundations.
  • Identify people and agent roles, information boundaries, permissions and review criteria.
  • Aim to leave with a useful business decision and a practical next step.
